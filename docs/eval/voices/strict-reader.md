## Faults

### austen / breakup (f3 v3 s4 n3 n2 t3 s4)

_The rewrite keeps the order and facts and gets some period rhythm, but it adds a feeling ('hated and detested'), uses awkward periphrases ('little instrument', 'companion in the house'), and falls into bathos. The notes are sparse and slightly inaccurate, and the lesson is useful but modest._

- **fidelity**: “that hated and detested phrase”: This adds a feeling the writer never stated. The writer says only that he said it. The narrator now actively hates the phrase, which is an invented emotional stance, not commentary on what is there.
- **fidelity**: “bending over his little instrument”: The writer's 'scrolling on his phone' becomes an odd euphemism. It also adds a faintly contemptuous 'little' that the writer did not feel.
- **fidelity**: “in a very few minutes he told me”: The writer gives no timing. This adds an incident detail, and it blurs the sequence of his being nervous and then speaking.
- **fidelity**: “said that it was very well”: The writer said 'okay', which is a flat assent. 'Very well' is acceptable, but it shifts the tone slightly toward a formal acquiescence.
- **fidelity**: “carrying my grief too far”: The roommate thought she was 'overreacting'. This is close but turns the judgement into one about grief and loses the sense of a reaction to the event.
- **voice**: “I knew at once what such a message could mean.”: The rewrite is in first person with modern incident details (coffee-house, ice-cream, text as 'wrote'). The Austen narrator is third-person and ironic, so this reads as period costume over a modern confession. The irony and the wit are thin.
- **natural**: “My companion in the house”: This is a stilted periphrasis for 'roommate'. It is neither Austen's usage nor clear, and 'companion' implies a paid or genteel companion.
- **natural**: “and said that it was very well, and walked home in the rain, and ate a whole pint of ice-cream”: A string of polysyndetic 'and's with 'a whole pint of ice-cream' is bathetic. The anachronism is unmediated and reads as unintended comedy.
- **natural**: “in the town”: The writer's 'downtown' becomes 'in the town', which is vague and drops the sense of a city centre.
- **notes**: “cites Northanger Abbey, ch. 6, ¶ 36”: This is a precise-looking citation for a generic reported-speech move. It is unlikely to be verifiable, and the note does not say what the move is.
- **notes**: “Liberty taken: The voice's dry naming of the old excuse”: The note calls 'hated and detested phrase' a verdict, not a new incident. In fact it adds a feeling to the narrator. The note understates the change.
- **notes**: “(no notes)”: Paragraphs 1 and 4 carry real changes (the 'companion' periphrasis, the concession, the semicolon structure) and receive no notes, so the notes are thin.
- **form**: “But it still hurt.”: The writer's short closing sentence is folded into a semicolon chain, 'but it hurt me still'. The built outline says the voice leaves the hurt standing in a short sentence. The rewrite did not do this, so the structure notes contradict the result.

The lesson also says 'nine quick, flat sentences' when the draft has more, so it misdescribes the text.
- **structure**: “the truth of the woman's place at last”: The outline places the sister reveal at the turn, but the rewrite reveals it in paragraph 1. The 'as the voice would build it' outline therefore does not match the rewrite.
