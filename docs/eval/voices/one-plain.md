## Faults

### austen / breakup (f2 v3 s2 n3 n2 t3 s4)

_The rewrite is thin pastiche: it keeps the sequence but invents Ryan's age, swaps roommate for friend, and misreports its liberties in the notes. Citations look fabricated, and the Austen voice is mostly surface archaism over the writer's plain sentences._

- **fidelity**: “When a man of three-and-twenty wished to speak, there was but one thing to be said”: Invents a fact (Ryan's age, twenty-three) that the writer never gave. The note claims no new fact was added, which is false.
- **fidelity**: “begging that we might speak”: The writer said Ryan 'said we needed to talk'. 'Begging' adds an emotional intensity and a supplicating stance that the original lacks.
- **fidelity**: “looking down at something in his hand”: Obscures the phone/scrolling. The sense is kept vaguely, but it is a riddle-like evasion and loses the writer's detail of him distracted.
- **fidelity**: “I only bowed my head and said I understood.”: The writer's 'nodded and said okay' is changed to 'understood'. This is a shift in meaning: she expresses comprehension, not mere acquiescence. 'Bowed' is also more submissive than 'nodded'.
- **fidelity**: “My friend thought I made too much of such a trifling affair”: The roommate is changed to 'friend', so a fact is lost. 'Trifling affair' also makes the roommate dismissive in a way the original (overreacting) did not say, and it undercuts the narrator's hurt.
- **fidelity**: “in the town”: The writer said 'downtown'. This is a minor loss, but 'coffee-house in the town' is also anachronistic muddle.
- **sense**: “there was but one thing to be said, and I had no doubt of what was coming.”: Redundant with the preceding clause, and 'there was but one thing to be said' is a vague generalisation that adds a claim about men's behaviour. It adds nothing to the sense and arguably inflates it.
- **natural**: “where he was already before me, looking down at something in his hand, and in a countenance that betrayed no little discomposure”: Clumsy syntax: 'looking... and in a countenance' joins a participle to a prepositional phrase awkwardly. 'Already before me' is also odd.
- **natural**: “I walked home afterwards in the rain, and ate a whole pint of ice cream.”: 'Pint of ice cream' is jarringly modern, and unrendered, and the line has no Austenian irony or framing. The rewrite is mostly modern prose here.
- **voice**: “But it still hurt.”: A flat, modern ending kept verbatim. Austen would not use 'hurt' this way; she would say 'wounded', 'pained', 'felt it'. The note calls this an 'ironic verdict', which it is not.
- **notes**: “cites Lady Susan, ch. 24, ¶ 3”: Source citations (Lady Susan ch. 24, Emma ch. 8 ¶ 58, Mansfield Park ch. 41 ¶ 16, etc.) are precise-looking but implausible or unverifiable. Lady Susan has letters rather than numbered chapters in this form, and paragraph numbers look fabricated. 'Speech reported' from Emma ch. 38 is an odd choice.
- **notes**: “a generalization the occasion invites, in the voice's manner, framing the narrator's instant knowledge; it adds no new fact”: The note misreports the change: the generalisation specifies a man of three-and-twenty, which is a new fact. The note is inaccurate and does not flag the age.
- **notes**: “Feeling shown by conduct: “I wished to cry, but I did not; I only bowed my head and said I understood.””: The writer already showed this by conduct, with 'I wanted to cry but I didn't'. Calling it a move of the voice is misleading, as only the verb ('wished') has changed.
- **teaching**: “letting an understatement (a 'not' where you said 'no') carry the feeling”: The lesson's parenthetical is garbled and not usable advice. 'No little discomposure' for 'nervous' also inflates the original rather than understating it. The outline's claim that the roommate 'holds her grief too great' and that 'it smarted' does not match the rewrite.
- **voice**: “Ryan wrote to me at midnight”: The modern name 'Ryan' and 'ice cream' sit beside archaic diction, so the result is costume rather than a true Austen register. There is little free indirect discourse, irony or social observation, and the sentences remain short and plain.

### austen / neighbors (f3 v3 s2 n3 n2 t3 s4)

_A competent, readable Regency-flavoured rewrite that keeps the story's order but misses its one real task. The mother/divorced-father contradiction is not truly mended, and the note explaining it is garbled. Several small fidelity slips (the invented 'three hours', 'sent' for 'baked', 'cart') and loosely labelled, doubtfully cited notes weaken it further._

- **sense**: “the lady of the house was rather reserved than otherwise”: The draft's contradiction is left standing. 'Lady of the house' still reads as the father's wife, yet the father is later 'single' and 'parted from his wife'. The woman is never made someone else in the household, such as a sister, mother or housekeeper, so the reader is still unsure who she is.
- **notes**: “I have made her no wife at all but rather the lady of the household in no such relation to him — rendered as her being reserved without any name of relation”: The sense note is garbled and does not describe a clear mending. It says the woman is 'no wife at all', but the text never states what she is. The writer cannot tell what was fixed or how to fix such a fault themselves.
- **fidelity**: “almost before they were three hours settled”: This invents a specific time. The writer said only 'right away'.
- **fidelity**: “had biscuits sent to them”: The writer's mother baked the cookies and brought them herself, which is why she could find out everything. 'Baked' is lost, and 'sent' changes the action and weakens the means of her inquiry.
- **fidelity**: “on the Saturday last”: The writer said 'last weekend'. A specific day is added.
- **fidelity**: “or rather, that he had parted from his wife”: 'Divorced' becomes 'parted', which could mean merely separated. This softens the fact, and the writer's 'Well, divorced' correction is turned into a vaguer one.
- **fidelity**: “I was not long in understanding her.”: The writer says they knew exactly what she was thinking, which is certainty. This reads as a gradual understanding and changes the sense. The ironic punch of the closing line is also flattened.
- **natural**: “a very large cart of their own”: A 'cart' for a moving truck is an odd, comic substitute. 'Of their own' is awkward. The semicolon before 'where they came from' is also unidiomatic.
- **natural**: “She has been trying to settle me for years.”: 'Settle me' is vague and slightly off. Austen would say 'to see me married' or 'to settle me in life', and the line lacks her irony.
- **voice**: “It was soon known that the father was a lawyer”: The passive is thin, and much of the rewrite stays plain and light on Austen's ironic narrator commentary. The style is mostly a gentle archaism: 'good sort of man' is not very Austen, and the mother's officiousness is barely weighed.
- **notes**: “Judgement in abstract nouns: “The father seemed a good sort of man, and the lady of the house was rather reserved than otherwise.” — cites Emma, ch. 1, ¶ 46”: The quoted sentence contains no abstract nouns, so the label does not match the text. The very specific paragraph citation looks unreliable.
- **notes**: “Understatement and litotes: “or rather, that he had parted from his wife.” — cites Emma, ch. 44, ¶ 1”: This is a euphemism or correction, not litotes. The label and citation are not apt.
- **notes**: “Clauses balanced in one period: “A new family had come to the house next door on the Saturday last, bringing with them a very large cart of their own, and two little children.””: The sentence is a participial list, not balanced clauses. The notes name moves the text barely performs.
- **teaching**: “Practise joining your short sentences into periods of twenty words and more”: The advice is generic and does not address the real lesson in this text, which is the sense fault. The notes' vague handling of it leaves the writer nothing to reuse.
