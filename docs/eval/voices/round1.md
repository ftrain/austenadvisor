## Faults

### austen / breakup (f3 v3 s4 n3 n2 t3 s4)

_The rewrite keeps the events and order and has some period flavour, but it shifts several feelings (nervous to 'out of spirits', 'okay' to 'all very well') and adds ironic commentary that is clumsy ('as everybody's conduct in such cases is'). The notes mislabel moves and cite suspect chapter/paragraph sources, and the lesson is thin._

- **fidelity**: “he looked not a little out of spirits”: The draft says Ryan looked nervous. 'Out of spirits' means low or dejected, so the feeling shifts from anxiety to gloom.
- **fidelity**: “as everybody's conduct in such cases is”: Adds a sweeping claim about everyone's behaviour that the writer never made. The grammar is also muddled: 'conduct' is made to match 'looking down at some little screen'.
- **natural**: “as everybody's conduct in such cases is”: The clause is clumsy and ungrammatical. It reads as forced irony and does not sound like Austen's smooth generalisations.
- **fidelity**: “with the air of one who felt the occasion to require it”: Invents a characterisation of Ryan, a theatrical air in delivering the line. The draft only says what he said, and this tilts the reader's judgement of him beyond it.
- **fidelity**: “said that it was all very well”: The draft has her say 'okay', a plain acceptance. 'All very well' carries a reservation or irony that changes the tone of her reply.
- **fidelity**: “I could have cried, but I did not”: 'I wanted to cry' becomes 'could have cried', which is a different thing: capacity, not desire.
- **fidelity**: “which was at least consistent with the occasion”: An added ironic verdict on her own indulgence. The meaning is also murky: what is consistent with what? It undercuts the hurt the draft is expressing.
- **natural**: “My companion in the house with me”: A laboured circumlocution for 'roommate'. Austen would say 'my companion' or 'the friend who lived with me'. It reads as a riddle.
- **natural**: “ate a whole pint of ice,”: 'A pint of ice' is odd and ambiguous, and the notes quote it as 'ice-cream'. The sentence also trails off on its tail clause.
- **voice**: “a coffee room in the town”: In Austen a coffee-room belongs to an inn. 'In the town' is a flat gloss of 'downtown'. The diction is period dress rather than period habit.
- **voice**: “and I knew, the moment I read his words, what their meaning was.”: The inverted ending is stiff. Austen's first-person narrators, and her narrator generally, are more fluent and ironic, so this sounds like pastiche.
- **notes**: “Judgement in abstract nouns: “he looked not a little out of spirits””: The quoted phrase contains no abstract-noun judgement, and it is cited again for litotes. The move label does not match the example.
- **notes**: “cites Lady Susan, ch. 24, ¶ 3”: Lady Susan is an epistolary novella arranged in letters, not chapters. The precise chapter and paragraph citations throughout look unverifiable or invented.
- **notes**: ““I then walked home in the rain, and ate a whole pint of ice-cream, which was at least consistent with the occasion.””: The note quotes 'ice-cream' and 'ice-cream' differently from the rewrite's 'ice'. The notes also never flag the substantive additions ('as everybody's conduct', 'with the air of one who...').
- **teaching**: “Practise joining clauses with semicolons and "and"”: The lesson is two sentences and generic. It gives no concrete before/after from this text and no reason why the moves work, and it does not mention the irony it relied on.

### austen / neighbors (f3 v3 s3 n3 n2 t3 s4)

_The rewrite keeps the story and order, and Austenish periods and diction are present. But the standoffish-woman fix is only half-resolved, one key sentence is garbled, a few things are added or altered without comment, and the notes have dubious citations and a mislabelled sense note._

- **fidelity**: “a large cart”: The writer's 'huge truck' is replaced by a period object, which changes the thing described. The notes do not mention it.
- **fidelity**: “and carried them over herself”: This adds an action the writer never gave. It is not reported as a liberty.
- **fidelity**: “it was soon agreed, was very pleasant”: The writer gave one narrator's impression ('seems nice'). The rewrite invents a consensus ('agreed') with no one named, and 'very pleasant' is stronger than 'seems nice'.
- **fidelity**: “a single man with a profession and two children, settled, as one might say, at her very door”: This adds the narrator's gloss and shifts the closing beat. The writer's final point was that the mother has tried to set her up 'for years'. The rewrite ends on the neighbour's proximity, which weakens that emphasis.
- **sense**: “The woman with him was of a reserved cast of countenance”: The mending is weak. 'The woman with him' still reads as a partner, so the contradiction with 'single' is only blurred, not resolved. The children's mother is left unaccounted for. The woman is never plainly identified as a sister, housekeeper or other relation.
- **natural**: “which must be met with all one's own civility, and would pay back none of it”: The grammar is garbled. A 'cast of countenance' is made the thing that 'would pay back' civility, and the sentence shifts between abstract and personal subjects. It reads as a riddle, and the harsher 'pay back none' overstates 'kind of standoffish'.
- **natural**: “parted from his wife”: 'Well, divorced' is a rueful correction. 'Parted from his wife' is vaguer and loses the writer's quick self-correction.
- **voice**: “A new family took possession of the next house at the close of the week”: The Austen cadence is imitated mainly through semicolons and 'and' chains. It lacks her characteristic ironic generalization and free indirect judgement, so it reads partly as period costume.
- **notes**: “cites Emma, ch. 29, ¶ 40”: The chapter and paragraph citations look unverifiable or invented, and they are not clearly apt for the moves claimed. The same applies to the Mansfield Park citations.
- **notes**: “Understatement and litotes: “a reserved cast of countenance, which must be met with all one's own civility, and would pay back none of it””: The quoted line is not litotes. It also reuses the sentence already cited for 'judgement in abstract nouns', which pads the notes.
- **notes**: “The passage truly echoes the opening of Pride and Prejudice ... The rewrite takes from that scene the mother's eager fact-finding”: This is labelled a sense note but mends nothing. It wrongly says the rewrite 'takes' material from Pride and Prejudice, when it only keeps the writer's own content. It also assumes the narrator is a daughter ('her mother'), which the draft does not state.
- **notes**: “which points the joke without adding any fact”: The liberty notes claim nothing is added. The rewrite does add inferred content ('settled ... at her very door') and the action of carrying the biscuits over, and these are unreported.
- **teaching**: “Practise holding several clauses in one sentence”: The lesson is a single, fairly generic syntax tip. The notes do not show concretely how to move from the writer's own short sentences to the Austen version, and the outline offers little beyond the writer's order.

### austen / oped (f2 v3 s1 n2 n2 t2 s3)

_The rewrite has an Austen flavour but distorts several meanings (strollers become 'carriages of another kind', and 'minds it not a little' inverts the feeling) and adds claims. The notes duplicate each other, and the citations are doubtful._

- **fidelity**: “close Main Street to carriages at the end of every week”: The writer wants cars closed out on weekends. Rendering 'cars' as 'carriages' is a period transposition, and 'the end of every week' blurs 'weekends'. The notes never mention the transposition.
- **sense**: “the pavements are far too narrow for carriages of another kind”: The writer's point is that sidewalks are too narrow for strollers. 'Carriages of another kind' is a riddle: it is not clear that it means prams. Since Main Street is closed to carriages, the sentence is also muddled. The mending is unreported.
- **fidelity**: “The placing of a carriage in the town is a situation of no small distress”: 'Parking downtown is a nightmare' is narrowed to the placing of a carriage 'in the town'. The word 'downtown' is lost, and 'no small distress' is an unclear stand-in for the nightmare.
- **fidelity**: “Places, indeed, there are which have done the same, and which find their shops in greater credit than they ever enjoyed before”: 'Other towns' becomes 'places', and 'doing better than ever' becomes 'in greater credit', which suggests reputation or borrowing rather than trade. The note calls this an 'ironic verdict', but nothing here is ironic.
- **fidelity**: “their guests cannot come at them”: 'Customers' becomes 'guests', which is a shift in meaning. 'Come at them' is awkward and could be read as an attack.
- **fidelity**: “and the inconvenience would be felt only till the habit was formed”: This is an added claim that the inconvenience is temporary, beyond the writer's 'they'll get used to it'. It also repeats the clause before it.
- **fidelity**: “most of the town walks from the stable already, and minds it not a little, as the surest means of reconciling themselves to it”: The writer said 'most people walk from the garage anyway'. The rewrite adds that they mind it, which is a new claim, and it is garbled: 'minds it not a little' means they mind it a great deal, which contradicts the writer's point that it's no hardship. The 'reconciling' clause is also incoherent. 'Stable' for 'garage' is also odd, since carriages would not be walked from a stable.
- **natural**: “minds it not a little, as the surest means of reconciling themselves to it”: The sentence is unclear and reads as an unintended joke or a malapropism. The litotes reverses the intended feeling.
- **voice**: “There is no reason why we should not make some trial of it this summer.”: This is a passable Austenian double negative, but it is flatter than the writer's direct call, and the passage is not otherwise Austen's irony or narrative stance.
- **notes**: “Liberty taken: The writer's 'they'll get used to it' and 'most people walk anyway' are framed as a general truth about habituation”: The two liberty notes duplicate each other. They also don't report the real liberties: carriage for car, guests for customers, the added 'minds it'.
- **notes**: “cites Lady Susan, ch. 19, ¶ 4”: Lady Susan has no such chapter structure, since it is epistolary. Several of the citations (Emma ch. 46 ¶17, Northanger Abbey ch. 22 ¶10) look fabricated or inapt. The note on 'minds it not a little' as litotes misreads the phrase.
- **teaching**: “Practise joining clauses with 'and' and preferring the abstract noun and the deflating reflection to words like 'nightmare.'”: The advice is generic. It does not warn that the draft's transposition obscured meaning, and the lesson follows a flawed rewrite, so the writer would copy the faults. The notes give no outline of the structure.
- **structure**: “For in truth, most of the town walks from the stable already”: The weak answer to the objection is muddled instead of being strengthened. The notes make no claim about structure.

The notes cite dubious chapter and paragraph sources and mislabel some moves._
