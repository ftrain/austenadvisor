## Faults

### austen / breakup (f3 v3 s4 n3 n2 t3 s4)

_The rewrite keeps the events and their order but adds an invented detail (he did not notice her), changes 'roommate' to 'friend', and leans on a strained 'as such messages generally do'. The Austen voice is partial and the notes are thin and unreliable._

- **fidelity**: “My friend was of opinion”: The writer's 'roommate' became 'friend'. The relationship is changed and a detail dropped.
- **fidelity**: “so busy over the instrument in his hand that he did not at first perceive me”: The writer only says he was scrolling on his phone. The rewrite adds that he did not notice her, which is a new incident beyond the source.
- **fidelity**: “and in the same breath professed that he was not ready for any serious attachment, and that the fault was not mine but his own”: 'In the same breath' ties his nervousness to the speech, which the source does not say. 'Attachment' also shifts 'serious' from the relationship to the feeling.
- **fidelity**: “as such messages generally do”: This adds a sardonic general judgement and a slightly arch tone that the writer's plain, hurt narrator does not have. The note admits it is added commentary, but it tips the feeling toward irony and away from the sincere hurt.
- **natural**: “as such messages generally do”: The phrase is awkward and nearly tautological ('such a message meant, as such messages do'). It reads as a clumsy pastiche, not a real Austen irony.
- **voice**: “Ryan sent me word at midnight that he must speak with me”: 'Sent me word' is period-appropriate. But the rewrite keeps the modern first name 'Ryan', 'ice-cream' and 'the business' in a thin costume. The narration lacks Austen's balanced periods and free indirect judgement, and the passage is mostly flat transposition.
- **voice**: “I only nodded and said that it was all right”: Reported speech is correct, but 'all right' is a modern idiom Austen would hardly use. 'Walked home in the rain and ate a whole pint of ice-cream' is likewise anachronistic and comic in this register.
- **natural**: “ate a whole pint of ice-cream”: The modern image sits uneasily in the period diction. The rewrite does nothing to bridge it, so the effect is unintentionally comic.
- **notes**: “cites Pride and Prejudice, ch. 33, ¶ 38”: The citation is precise-looking but unconvincing as support for this move. Ch. 18, ¶ 9 for 'feeling shown by conduct' is similarly doubtful. The notes also skip the changes to 'coffee house', 'instrument', 'my friend' and the added 'did not perceive me'. Paragraphs 2 and 4 get no notes even though they hold the largest liberties.
- **teaching**: “I joined the three opening sentences into a single period”: The lesson claims a joined period, but it is only a loosely conjoined sentence with a tag. The advice about 'main point arrives late' is not borne out by the actual sentence, and nothing explains the invented 'instrument' detail.
- **fidelity**: “but it hurt me notwithstanding”: This slightly softens the writer's flat 'it still hurt'. 'Notwithstanding' is stiff, and 'perhaps I did' follows the 'overreacting' doubt in a looser way than the original.

### austen / camp-letter (f3 v3 s4 n3 n1 t3 s4)

_A competent Regency-flavoured rewrite with an apt lesson, but it adds small facts and feelings, garbles the canoe, 'country of Ohio' and the charger periphrasis, and leaves nearly all of its moves unannotated. The one citation looks invented._

- **fidelity**: “went upon the water in a little boat, and, in turning it, I was overturned into the lake”: The canoe becomes 'a little boat', and 'flipped the canoe' becomes 'in turning it', which is not the same event. 'Into the lake' and Jake's own fall are added; the original only says he flipped the canoe.
- **fidelity**: “so violently as to keep me from my rest”: The writer said only that the bunkmate snores like crazy. Lost sleep is a new consequence.
- **fidelity**: “laughed without mercy”: 'Without mercy' sharpens the feeling beyond 'everybody laughed'.
- **fidelity**: “whom I like very much indeed, for she is from the country of Ohio, and knows more of horses than anybody I ever met with”: 'For' makes Ohio and horses the reason he likes her, which the writer did not say. 'Knows a ton' becomes a superlative claim ('more than anybody I ever met').
- **fidelity**: “talk of everything in the world”: 'Talk about stuff' is casual and vague, and this inflates it.
- **fidelity**: “and we are all very well”: This is an invented assurance of general health that the writer never gave.
- **natural**: “the country of Ohio”: Ohio is a state, so this reads as a slip or unintended comedy.
- **natural**: “a little more in the way of eating”: This is an awkward, vague circumlocution for 'snacks'.
- **natural**: “I have no very great complaint to make of it”: It sits oddly before an immediate list of complaints, so the sentence reads as slightly self-contradictory.
- **natural**: “the cord for my little instrument of talking at a distance”: The periphrasis is so heavy that it becomes a joke, and it is a modern anachronism put in period dress. 'Cord' also loses 'charger'.
- **notes**: “cites Pride and Prejudice, ch. 31, ¶ 25”: The citation looks fabricated or unverifiable. Nothing in that passage is a periphrasis for a gadget, so the source is not apt.
- **notes**: “Paragraph 1:
  (no notes)”: Real changes go unnamed: 'okay I guess' to 'tolerable upon the whole', the litotes, 'mortification enough', 'a Kayla', the 'at which' construction and the formal signoff. Only one move is noted, and the notes do not match the rewrite's four paragraphs.
- **voice**: “Your affectionate son, Jake.”: The sign-off is plausible, but the whole letter is stiffly formal for a young camper's letter. It leans on stock Regency phrases ('upon the whole', 'Pray', 'very well') more than on Austen's irony, so it reads as costume in places.

### austen / job-interview (f3 v4 s4 n3 n3 t3 s3)

_The rewrite gets Austen's balanced periods and ironic generalisation, and it is mostly readable. But it drops 'I really need this job', adds an invented worry, inflates the sister's 'probably' into 'no doubt', and uses a non-Austen 'essentially'. The notes also under-report these changes._

- **fidelity**: “and could only hope that my countenance did not betray it”: Invented content. The writer never says the narrator worried about hiding the discomfort. This is added anxiety, not commentary on what is there.
- **fidelity**: “and I hope she is right, for the rent falls due next week, and I am essentially without money”: The writer's 'I really need this job' is dropped. That is the emotional point of the passage, and the structure notes themselves call it the point of the whole. The rent and being broke are kept, but the need for the job is gone.
- **fidelity**: “she had no doubt I had performed it to admiration”: The sister's hedged 'probably nailed it' becomes certainty ('no doubt'), which intensifies the feeling beyond the writer's. 'To admiration' also overstates 'nailed it'.
- **fidelity**: “of a manner somewhat too eager”: 'Intense' becomes 'eager', which is a different quality. The 'too' also adds a mild criticism the writer did not make.
- **fidelity**: “wore the loose coarse dress of the place”: 'Hoodies' is rendered as 'coarse', which adds a disparaging judgement. The casual-versus-suit contrast survives only vaguely.
- **natural**: “I am essentially without money”: 'Essentially' is a modern hedge used for 'basically', not in Austen's sense. The phrase reads awkwardly and weakens the stakes.
- **natural**: “the loose coarse dress of the place”: 'Coarse' is odd for a description of casual clothing, and the phrase obscures what is meant.
- **natural**: “which is, as every body who has ever been asked it knows, the confession of one who has nothing better to say”: Heavy and slightly confused. The long insertion before the main predicate makes the dry verdict clumsy.
- **notes**: “cites Pride and Prejudice, ch. 43, ¶ 5”: The citations give precise chapter and paragraph numbers for loose parallels. They look unverifiable and over-precise as support for the moves.
- **notes**: “the voice's one ironic liberty, and the passage's single such framing”: The notes do not report the other additions and shifts: the 'countenance' clause, 'eager' for 'intense', 'no doubt' for 'probably', and the dropped need for the job. They claim fewer liberties than were taken.
- **structure**: “I hope she is right, for the rent falls due next week”: The writer's closing beat, 'I really need this job', is gone. The close turns into a bare financial remark, so the intended emotional close is lost. The tense also drifts into the present.
- **teaching**: “the need arriving last as the point of the whole”: The lesson and build notes praise the need as the closing point, but the rewrite omitted it. The teaching does not match the output.

### austen / neighbors (f2 v3 s4 n3 n2 t3 s3)

_The rewrite correctly catches and mends the mom/divorced contradiction and has a passable Austenian surface. But it invents incidents (the mother's lingering, the sister who keeps house), leaves some modern phrasing, and its notes include inaccurate claims about Austen and a doubtful citation._

- **fidelity**: “his sister, who keeps his house, was of a colder cast”: The writer said 'the mom'. The rewrite makes her a sister and adds that she keeps his house. The mend was needed, but the housekeeping detail is an invention beyond what the mending required, and it makes her the father's sister rather than leaving her an unspecified woman.
- **fidelity**: “she did not return till she had satisfied herself on every one of these points”: This invents an incident: the mother's long stay and her satisfaction. The writer only said she wanted to find out everything.
- **fidelity**: “almost before the family had done taking out”: This adds a detail about timing and unloading. The writer's 'right away' is kept, but the phrase is clumsy and elliptical, with an object missing after 'taking out'.
- **fidelity**: “at the end of last week”: The writer said 'last weekend'. The phrase shifts the timing slightly.
- **fidelity**: “that is to say, he had been parted from his wife”: This is a reasonable rendering of 'divorced'. It also reintroduces a wife, which sits oddly beside the sister who now fills the mom's place and is not explained.
- **natural**: “a large cart”: A 'huge truck' becomes a 'large cart'. The substitution is period-flavored but loses the writer's emphasis, and a cart for a lawyer's household is odd.
- **natural**: “took a look which I had too often seen to mistake”: The syntax is slightly strained: 'took a look' is an awkward verb phrase for a countenance, and the sentence leans on an unidiomatic construction.
- **natural**: “who keeps his house, was of a colder cast”: The present tense 'keeps' sits beside the past 'was', and the tenses are mixed.
- **voice**: “I knew exactly what she was thinking.”: This is left in plain modern phrasing. 'Exactly' is not Austen's register and it reads as modern prose in costume.
- **notes**: “The voice lets the narrator, looking back, judge the mother's scheme dryly in a brief closing clause, as Austen's children-narrators report a parent's designs”: Austen has no children-narrators. The claim is inaccurate, and no closing clause was added, so the note describes nothing.
- **notes**: “cites Sense and Sensibility, ch. 22, ¶ 40”: The citation is unlikely to support the specific sentence. It is vague and probably not verifiable, and it is attached to a move that is mostly the rewriter's own invention.
- **notes**: “Paragraph 2:
  (no notes)”: The second paragraph made notable changes (invented satisfaction, 'biscuits' for cookies, 'could never rest till') but none are noted.
- **notes**: “the biscuits standing in for the visit of introduction”: This overclaims Austen's practice and mislabels what the rewrite does. The Mrs Bennet comparison is aptly drawn, but the claim that she 'acts only on a confirmed single man' is a loose reading.
- **sense**: “the household keeps a second adult, the narrator's mother still has someone to receive her”: The mending rationale is partly speculative and the 'someone to receive her' point is invented. The mend itself is acceptable, but it is justified with reasoning the text does not support.
- **teaching**: “Most of my sentences join two of yours with a semicolon”: This is inaccurate: the rewrite has few semicolon joins. The lesson is partly generic, and 'settle' is not shown to be a transferable move.

### austen / oped (f2 v2 s1 n2 n2 t2 s3)

_The rewrite swaps in period words mechanically and produces real confusions: 'carriages of children' beside banned carriages, and 'carriage-house' for garage. It is thinly Austenian and keeps most of the modern sentence shapes. The notes and lesson misdescribe or praise the mistakes._

- **sense**: “the pavements are too narrow for the carriages of children”: 'Strollers' becomes 'carriages of children', yet 'carriages' is also the word for the vehicles being banned. The sentence now confuses the two senses. Closing the street to carriages would also seem to help the narrow pavements less than it should.
- **sense**: “Most people walk from the carriage-house already”: A carriage-house is a private outbuilding for storing carriages, not a public garage. The transposition is a mistranslation, and walking from one's own carriage-house to Main Street makes little sense for the argument.
- **fidelity**: “Our town should close Main Street to carriages on the days of rest.”: 'Weekends' becomes 'days of rest', which is Sunday only. This is a shift in meaning, and 'Our town should close' is left in modern phrasing.
- **fidelity**: “To put a carriage anywhere in the town is a thing of no small difficulty”: The writer said downtown parking is a nightmare. This turns it into a difficulty of putting a carriage anywhere in the whole town, which weakens the intensity and widens the scope.
- **fidelity**: “Most people walk from the carriage-house already, in truth.”: The writer's 'Honestly' marks a tone of candid impatience. 'In truth' is a tacked-on tag. The wry dismissiveness of 'they'll get used to it' is also somewhat flattened.
- **voice**: “their shopkeepers are doing better than they have ever done before”: This is wordy, and 'ever' and 'before' are redundant. It is not Austen's economy, and it does not read as her phrasing. The first paragraph's opening is also still the writer's modern sentence, with no narrator's irony or social judgement.
- **voice**: “We should make the trial this summer.”: This is a near-direct copy of the modern sentence. Austen's cadence, with its qualifying clauses and formal turns, is absent; it reads as modern prose in costume.
- **notes**: “cites Emma, ch. 3, ¶ 5”: The citations to Emma and Persuasion are specific but unverifiable and look invented. The notes claim 'balanced clauses' where the sentence is just two joined clauses.
- **notes**: “the voice strengthens it so the improvement sounds the greater, a small intensifying liberty”: The rewrite does not actually strengthen 'better than ever'; it is a plain equivalent.
