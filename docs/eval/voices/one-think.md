## Faults

### austen / breakup (f3 v3 s3 n3 n2 t2 s4)

_A competent but thin Austen pastiche with a few modern-sounding or altered details (instrument, roommate to friend, 'all very well'). The notes include an inaccurate liberty and doubtful citations, and the lesson is generic._

- **fidelity**: “with his eyes fixed upon a little instrument in his hand”: The writer's 'scrolling on his phone' is turned into a riddling periphrasis. The activity of scrolling is lost and the 'instrument' is unclear.
- **fidelity**: “it was therefore with no very great surprise that I heard him say”: This adds a feeling the writer did not state, that she was not surprised at the meeting. It also makes 'therefore' derive her expectation from his nervousness, which shifts the causation.
- **fidelity**: “said that it was all very well”: The writer's 'okay' becomes a phrase that implies mild irony or grudging acceptance, which changes her tone. It reads as sarcastic or dismissive.
- **fidelity**: “My friend thought I made too much of it”: 'Roommate' is changed to 'friend', a fact altered. 'Overreacting' is softened.
- **fidelity**: “and perhaps I did, but it hurt me still”: The writer's 'But it still hurt' is kept, but the closing beat is flattened by running into the same sentence as the friend's judgement, which weakens the contrast.
- **fidelity**: “He said we needed to talk.”: This is rendered 'no more than that we must talk', adding 'no more than', a slight embellishment of what he said.
- **voice**: “ate a whole pint of ice-cream”: Ice-cream by the pint, together with 'coffee-house' and a walk home in the rain, sits in modern idiom within Regency diction. The mix reads as costume. The voice's irony and free indirect discourse are thin, and the paragraph openings are plain.
- **voice**: “in the town”: 'Downtown' becomes a vague phrase that sounds odd in Austen. A name or a more characteristic phrase would suit better.
- **natural**: “he was already there before me”: 'already' and 'before me' are redundant.
- **notes**: “The narrator adds a dry understatement of her own composure, which the opening had already earned”: This liberty note describes an addition that is not in the rewrite. No such composure sentence exists, so the note is inaccurate and confusing.
- **notes**: “cites Lady Susan, ch. 24, ¶ 3”: The citations to chapters and paragraphs look precise, but their aptness is doubtful. Lady Susan is an epistolary novella with letters, not a source for narrator balance. The numbers look fabricated.
- **notes**: “Ironic verdict: “My friend thought I made too much of it”: The cited move overstates the irony, since the line is mild. Changing 'roommate' to 'friend' is not reported as a liberty.
- **teaching**: “Practise joining short sentences with semicolons and ", and"”: The advice is generic and mechanical. It does not explain why Austen's periods work or show a reusable argumentative or emotional technique. The semicolon-chaining in the rewrite is also fairly uniform.
