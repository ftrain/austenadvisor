## Faults

### austen / breakup (f3 v3 s3 n2 n2 t3 s4)

_The rewrite keeps the order and has some convincing Austen periods, but 'a pint of ice', 'his instrument' and the muddled added clauses are garbled or comic, and the added 'composure' changes the writer's meaning. The notes are bloated and their citations are doubtful, though the lesson offers some transferable moves._

- **natural**: “ate a whole pint of ice before I could think the matter over”: 'Ice cream' is cut to 'ice', so the line reads as a pint of plain ice. This is garbled and unintentionally comic, and it blurs the comfort-eating the writer meant.
- **natural**: “with his instrument in his hand”: 'Instrument' for a phone is a riddle, not Austen's idiom. A reader would take it for a musical instrument. The periphrasis obscures rather than wittily veils.
- **fidelity**: “with his instrument in his hand”: The writer's detail that he was 'scrolling' is lost, so the image of him absorbed in his phone while waiting is gone.
- **fidelity**: “as if the want of readiness had belonged to himself rather than to his circumstances”: This added clause is muddled and its meaning is hard to follow. It inserts a judgement the writer did not make and blunts the plain 'it wasn't me, it was him'. The notes call it a light liberty, but it is obscure and heavy.
- **fidelity**: “before I could think the matter over with any composure”: This adds a new motive and inner state (she needed composure to reflect) that the writer never gave. It softens the raw, unreflective grief of the original.
- **fidelity**: “said it was all right”: 'Okay' becomes 'all right', which sounds more like granting absolution, and a flat 'okay' is not that. It is a small shift in the degree of her acquiescence.
- **natural**: “but it hurt still, for all that”: The word order 'hurt still' is stiff, and the dash before it is modern punctuation. The closing line, which should land hardest, is weakened. It also does not answer the roommate more pointedly, as the structure outline recommended.
- **fidelity**: “My companion, who shared my room”: 'Roommate' becomes someone who shares her bedroom. This is a slightly different relationship, and 'companion' loses the household sense.
- **notes**: “Of the Austen scenes listed, the passage genuinely echoes none closely”: No scenes were listed. The note rambles, hedges and disclaims its own relevance (Hunsford, Box Hill, the opening of Pride and Prejudice), and it is filed as a 'sense' note when it concerns source-matching. It is confusing for a learner.
- **notes**: “cites Pride and Prejudice, ch. 29, ¶ 28”: The chapter and paragraph citations (ch. 29, ch. 31, ch. 18) look unreliable or invented. They do not clearly match the named moves, and the 'instrument' periphrasis is not demonstrably from ch. 31.
- **notes**: “(no notes)”: Paragraphs 1 and 4 have no notes, though the joined sentence and the roommate's verdict are the main changes. The lesson explains the first, but the notes never name it.
- **voice**: “we must speak together, and from such a summons, at such an hour”: The opening is a good Austen period, but elsewhere the voice slips into plain modern phrasing ('I only nodded', 'in the rain'). It lacks Austen's ironic narratorial judgement, which the outline said the piece should carry.

### austen / neighbors (f2 v3 s2 n2 n2 t3 s3)

_The rewrite catches the draft's contradiction but mends it into a new one, making the father's sister the children's mother. It also invents time and detail, and the notes quote text that is not in the rewrite. The Austen cadence is passable, but the sense error and clumsy sentences undermine it._

- **fidelity**: “about a fortnight ago”: The writer said 'last weekend'. The rewrite changes the time of the arrival for no reason.
- **fidelity**: “having been some years parted from his wife”: The writer says only 'divorced'. 'Some years' is an invented detail.
- **fidelity**: “her present civility was only the beginning of them”: The writer has no such claim. It adds the mother's present visit as the opening move of a campaign, which is more than 'she has been trying to set me up for years'.
- **fidelity**: “they keep a broad cart”: 'Huge truck' becomes 'broad', which loses the sense of size and bulk. 'Keep' also implies ownership of a cart rather than the arrival of a moving load.
- **sense**: “the children's mother, who lives in the house with him, is by no means so easy to know; being, as it proved, his sister”: It makes the father's sister the mother of the children he is father to. That is absurd, and it is a new contradiction in place of the old. The mending should make her an aunt or housekeeper, or drop 'mother'.
- **natural**: “who lives in the house with him ... who keeps his house for him”: The two clauses say nearly the same thing. 'Who lives in the house with him' also implies a wife before the correction arrives, so the sentence stumbles over its own reveal.
- **natural**: “is by no means so easy to know; being, as it proved, his sister”: The semicolon followed by a participle is clumsy. The mending is tacked on as an afterthought instead of arriving as news.
- **form**: “The father, who is a lawyer, seems a very good sort of man”: The lawyer fact is moved forward, so the 'turn' of the lawyer being single no longer pays off the mother's inquiries as the notes' own outline says it should. The later sentence then reduces the news to 'single'.
- **notes**: “'the father is a lawyer, and single; that is, divorced; the children's mother being his sister, who keeps his house.'”: The notes quote a mending sentence that does not appear in the rewrite, so they do not describe what was done. They also repeat the 'children's mother = his sister' error without noticing it.
- **notes**: “cites Northanger Abbey, ch. 12, ¶ 17”: The cited source does not plausibly contain this balanced-clause pattern, and the note is attached to a sentence that is the rewriter's own invention. The citation looks decorative.
- **notes**: “but not Mrs Bennet's vulgarity of speech (this mother is only reported, never quoted) nor the married-daughters anxiety”: This note describes things the rewrite does not do, and it is a rationale rather than a named change. The sense notes are otherwise muddled.
- **voice**: “a look passed over her face that wanted no translation”: The phrase is competent pastiche, but the rest is plain narration in the first person. It has little of Austen's irony or judging abstractions, and 'laying her plans for me in that quarter' is mildly modern in rhythm.
- **teaching**: “"The father, who is a lawyer, seems a very good sort of man, and the children's mother ... is by no means so easy to know"”: The lesson holds up as its model the very sentence that contains the sense error, so the writer learns from a flawed example. The lesson also does not explain how to mend a contradiction in a draft.

### austen / email (f2 v3 s3 n2 n2 t2 s3)

_The rewrite keeps the date change, the Wednesday deadline and the 'tell me today' request, but it invents clauses ('some may think no great evil', 'too late to set the matter right'), blurs 'launch' and 'pricing page' into riddles, and swaps the thanks for esteem. The notes are thin and partly inaccurate, and the lesson is generic and inconsistent with the text._

- **fidelity**: “a change which some may think no great evil, but which I cannot consent to make without the sanction of those whose business it is to advise us”: Invents a speculative clause, that some may think the delay no great evil, which the writer never said. It also turns 'I don't want us shipping without it' into a more elaborate statement of authority.
- **fidelity**: “when it may be too late to set the matter right”: Adds a consequence the writer did not state. Sam only asks to be told today rather than tomorrow, not that tomorrow would be too late.
- **fidelity**: “I am, with great esteem, your obliged friend, Sam.”: Replaces the plain thanks with warm esteem and 'obliged friend'. The register of feeling shifts and 'Thanks' is lost.
- **fidelity**: “The gentlemen of the law have not yet given their consent to the terms of our page”: 'Legal' becomes 'gentlemen of the law', and 'the pricing page' becomes 'the terms of our page'. This blurs that the pricing page is the item awaiting sign-off, so 'pricing' is effectively lost.
- **natural**: “the matter we have been preparing is to be set up on Thursday”: 'Launch' is vague and 'set up' is a wrong verb for it. 'The thing sent' later is also unclear, so what is being launched is a riddle.
- **natural**: “I have a piece of information for you”: This is clumsy and stiff, closer to costume than to Austen's way of opening.
- **natural**: “Let your several parts of the work be finished”: 'The deck' is rendered as 'the work', so the concrete object is lost. The imperative phrasing is also stiff.
- **notes**: “The voice renders the writer's 'I don't want us shipping without it' as a dry verdict on the delay itself, turning the complaint into the irony of a man who will not move before the lawyers have spoken”: The notes mislabel it as a 'complaint' and describe irony that is not in the text. They also omit that a speculative clause was added, and 'adds no incident' is false.
- **notes**: “cites Pride and Prejudice, ch. 19, ¶ 8”: The cited move, 'judgement in abstract nouns', does not match a sign-off line. The citation is dubious and the labelled move is not evident in the quoted words. The Mansfield Park citation is likewise doubtful.
- **notes**: “Paragraph 1:
  (no notes)”: The opening address and the heavy rewording of the first paragraph go unannotated, and there is no note on the Hi team → My dear friends change.
- **teaching**: “Practise joining your short sentences with "and" and "rather than" so the main point arrives last”: The advice is generic. It also misdescribes the rewrite, where the main point (the date change) comes first. The lesson claims 'turns a deadline into a favour', which is not true of the text.
- **form**: “My dear friends,”: Too intimate and archaic for a team email. 'Hi team' becomes a salutation that shifts the relationship, and the sign-off reads as a letter rather than a work email.
- **form**: “I am, with great esteem, your obliged friend, Sam.”: This sign-off is not warranted by the source. The how_it_is_built claim that Austen 'closes with thanks and the name' contradicts the rewrite, which drops the thanks.

### austen / oped (f3 v3 s3 n2 n2 t3 s3)

_The rewrite keeps the argument's elements and order, but it contains a real error ("readers" for customers), a confusing double use of "carriage", and a modern closing line with a redundant repetition. The notes cite an inapt Austen source, describe a reordering that did not occur, and the lesson miscounts the writer's reasons._

- **fidelity**: “their readers cannot get to them”: The writer said "customers". Shopkeepers have customers or patrons, not readers. This is an error of meaning, not an Austenian turn.
- **natural**: “their readers cannot get to them”: "Readers" is a malapropism for a shopkeeper's trade and reads as unintended comedy.
- **sense**: “closed to carriages ... too narrow for a child's carriage”: "Carriage" now means both the vehicles being banned and the strollers. The reader must work out which is meant, and the ban reads as if it might cover the child's carriage too.
- **fidelity**: “To leave a carriage in the town is now a great inconvenience”: The writer's "nightmare" is flattened to a mild "inconvenience", which changes the degree of the complaint. Austenian understatement can be defended here, but the notes do not flag it.
- **natural**: “from the place where the carriages stand”: This is a clumsy periphrasis for "the garage". It is wordy and vague, and it suggests a coach-yard rather than a parking structure.
- **structure**: “the trial might be made this very summer ... We should try it this summer.”: The summer trial is stated in the opening sentence and again in the closing line. The close is redundant and gains nothing by repeating it.
- **voice**: “We should try it this summer.”: This last line is left in plain modern phrasing. The "We should" construction is not Austen's, and it breaks the register of the rest.
- **voice**: “Other places have tried the same thing, and their shops have never been so well frequented.”: The diction is period-flavoured, but the rewrite has no ironic or evaluative narratorial stance, no balanced periods and no social observation. It reads as modern prose lightly dressed in period words.
- **notes**: “The nearest Austen scene is a claim about a town's arrangements pressed on its neighbours with reasons and an example (the opening of Pride and Prejudice), but it is a proposal of marriage and a scheme”: The cited source is inapt and incoherent. The opening of Pride and Prejudice is a universal truth about single men and fortune, not a town proposal or a proposal of marriage. The note also contradicts itself by admitting the echo is faint.
- **notes**: “Your paragraph 2, told after your paragraph 3: the telling reaches it later.”: The rewrite keeps the order 1, 2, 3, so there is no reordering to report. The note describes a change that did not happen, and the other structure notes are equally confusing.
- **notes**: “Brings together your paragraphs 1 and 3.”: The notes do not name the real moves: the "to speak the truth" concession, the "perhaps" hedge, the understatement, and the semicolon-joined reply. The sense note spends its length on an irrelevant source.
- **teaching**: “Your passage states a claim and three loose reasons”: The passage has two reasons, plus evidence. The lesson misdescribes the writer's text, which weakens its authority. The how_it_is_built also asserts unsupported claims about what Austen "never" does.

- **fidelity**: “I confess I have long had it in my head that it would answer”: Adds a claim that the speaker has long held this view. The writer says only 'I think'.
- **fidelity**: “as is often the way when men are over-worked”: Adds a generalization that is not in the draft. Notes call it a narrator's liberty, but a first-person pitch has no narrator.
- **fidelity**: “our best man in that line has just given notice and is gone”: 'Best engineer' becomes 'best man in that line', which blurs the role. 'Given notice and is gone' also changes 'quit' by doubling it.
- **fidelity**: “I have seen it stated, I believe — by which the work done is not lessened but rather increased”: The writer says productivity 'stays the same or goes up', so 'not lessened but rather increased' drops the 'same' case. 'I have seen it stated, I believe' weakens the claim to hearsay, and 'studies' is lost.
- **fidelity**: “which, if it be true, must dispose of the only objection of any weight”: Invents an argument the writer did not make, and ranks the objection ('the only objection of any weight') against the writer's own admission of risk in paragraph 2.

- **natural**: “Our people are worn out with their work”: 'Worn out with their work' is fine, but the following clause 'as is often the way when men are over-worked' makes the sentence clumsy, and 'and is gone' reads awkwardly.
- **notes**: “Fainter echo of the opening of Pride and Prejudice: a scheme for the people weighed by counting the cost aloud”: The echo claim is vague and does not match the opening of Pride and Prejudice. It is also filed under 'sense' though it is a move.

- **teaching**: “naming feeling by understatement, "worn out with their work,"”: The lesson claims understatement, but 'worn out' is already direct. The lesson also says the two closing sentences were joined, whereas the rewrite uses one sentence in that paragraph.
- **structure**: “But we might make the trial for three months, and then judge of the wisdom of continuing it.”: The outline claims the objection is stated 'at its strongest' and that the risk is placed before the advantage, yet the rewrite keeps the original order. The outline and the rewrite disagree.

### austen / relieved (f2 v3 s2 n3 n2 t2 s3)

_The rewrite is in a plausible Austenian register but invents an incident ('told so'), adds a claim about concealment, and leaves the final clause confused. The notes and lesson do not report these changes and praise the flawed ending._

- **fidelity**: “and was not sorry to be told so”: Invents a new incident: Dana telling the narrator they will not reschedule. In the original the narrator alone doubts they will. This adds a fact and changes who holds the judgment.
- **sense**: “and was not sorry to be told so”: The clause has no clear subject or sense. 'Told so' by whom? The sentence now contradicts the earlier 'I do not think that we shall', which was the narrator's own opinion. It also reads as a grammatical slip, since 'was' follows 'I do not think' with the subject dropped.
- **fidelity**: “and did not trouble myself to conceal it”: Adds a new fact, that the narrator did not hide her relief. This conflicts with the polite 'of no consequence' she told Dana and with the shame she feels. It is not the voice commenting on what is there.
- **fidelity**: “I was not at all sorry for it; on the contrary, I was relieved”: The writer says 'wasn't upset', not 'not sorry'. This is a small shift, and the 'on the contrary' stresses the relief more than the original does.
- **voice**: “scratched our dinner”: 'Scratched' is a modern or colloquial verb for cancelling, and not Austen's idiom. It jars against the formal register around it.
- **notes**: “Liberty taken: The writer says only that she was relieved and not upset; the voice, looking back, frames that relief in litotes”: Misdescribes the changes. It does not report the added 'did not trouble myself to conceal it' or the invented 'told so'. It calls litotes a liberty, which is not the real liberty.
- **notes**: “Judgement in abstract nouns: “I told her that it was of no consequence” — cites Pride and Prejudice, ch. 10, ¶ 17”: The citation is dubious and not shown to match. 'Of no consequence' is a stock phrase, not an abstract-noun judgement. Paragraph 1 has no notes even though it carries the main changes.
- **teaching**: “closed with an ironic turn ("was not sorry to be told so")”: The lesson praises as a model the very clause that invents a fact and muddles the sense. It also claims the rewrite kept 'I am not proud of having done so', which is a clumsy phrase. The writer would learn a flawed move.
- **structure**: “but I do not think that we shall, and was not sorry to be told so”: The close is meant to end on the flat verdict. The added clause blunts it and breaks the writer's ending, though the notes say the structure was kept.
- **natural**: “I am not proud of having done so”: 'Done so' refers loosely to dreading, which is a feeling and not an act. It is awkward, and 'the whole week' is stiff.

### kjv / neighbors (f2 v3 s3 n3 n2 t3 s3)

- **fidelity**: “his sister is aloof”: The mending invents a sister. The writer's 'mom' is made an aunt who lives with the household, a new fact. The mending is reported, but it is an invention that could have been avoided or marked more lightly. The children's 'mom' is also lost.
- **fidelity**: “and he was alone: he was put away from his wife”: 'Well, divorced' is the writer's self-correction, a comic beat. 'Put away from his wife' loses the hesitation and suggests the wife was cast off, shifting blame onto the dad. It also adds a moral judgment.
- **fidelity**: “cakes”: The writer's 'cookies' becomes 'cakes', a shift in the object. It is acceptable for the era, but not noted as a liberty.
- **fidelity**: “at the end of the week that is past”: 'Last weekend' becomes the end of the week; this is slightly imprecise. 'Now there came' also adds an opening not in the original.
- **fidelity**: “And my mother's countenance changed, and I knew just what she thought.”: The writer's 'look' is not necessarily a change of countenance. Also dropped is the 'got this look' sense of knowing exactly; 'just' is weak for 'exactly'. The 'kind of' softening of 'standoffish' is lost with 'aloof'.
- **voice**: “And their father seemeth a good man”: 'Seemeth' is a thin archaism, and 'seemeth a good man' is not KJV usage. The 'nice' sense is flattened to 'good' (moral rather than pleasant). 'Hath sought to set me in marriage these many years' is serviceable but pastiche; 'wrought for their bread' is apt.
- **voice**: “And it came to pass that the father was a lawyer”: 'It came to pass' is used for an event, not a discovery of fact; the KJV uses it for occurrences, so it is misapplied. 'Lawyer' is also used in the KJV, but in a different sense (expert in the law).
- **natural**: “he was put away from his wife”: In the KJV, 'put away' refers to the husband divorcing the wife (Matt 5:32). Here it reads as though the husband was put away by her, which is a role inversion and a malapropism.
- **natural**: “whether they went up unto the house of the LORD”: 'Went up' is a slightly forced pilgrimage idiom for ordinary church-going, which is a mild comic mismatch. It is arguably acceptable.
- **sense**: “No mending beyond the household: the final sentence supplies the answer, and the 'divorced' words supply the lawyer detail as the mother's measure of a prospect.”: Muddled note. The mending of the contradiction itself is reasonable, but this note reads as garbled and invents a rationale about the lawyer detail.
- **notes**: “True echo of Ruth 1 — a newcomer is taken in and questioned”: Ruth 1 is not about a neighbor's welcome with questions; it is a stretched, mislabelled source, and this note is a 'sense' note rather than a move. The Ezekiel 3:11, John 7:27, 2 Kings 15:12 and 1 Samuel 4:21 citations for single phrases are loose: they do not model the cited constructions.
- **notes**: “The voice frames the wariness the writer named ('standoffish') as a settled manner in the person, a verdict in its own words; the writer's own word has no equivalent in the King James Bible.”: The liberty note is vague and does not match the actual change (the mom became a sister and 'standoffish' became 'aloof'). The final-paragraph liberty note misdescribes 'just' for 'exactly' as a voice move.
- **teaching**: “I opened nearly every sentence with "And"”: The lesson is sound on and-joining, but the claim that the short sentences were joined into longer clauses is only partly true, since the rewrite stays choppy.
