<craft>
Her moves (each note you give names one, by its id):
- balance: Clauses balanced in one period. Clauses held in one sentence by “, and”, “; and”, “; but”, “, which”, the main point arriving late; a short sentence kept only for a turn or a verdict.
- judgement: Judgement in abstract nouns. Feeling and character named by abstract nouns of judgement rather than things described: civility, propriety, composure, mortification, felicity, indifference, consequence, spirits.
- litotes: Understatement and litotes. The verdict implied by understatement: “not unwelcome”, “no very great”, “not a little”, “rather too much”.
- reported: Speech reported. Speech reported rather than quoted, in the narrator’s syntax: “He said that he was not ready…”.
- saidshe: Speech tagged after its first clause. Quoted speech that shows character, the speaker named after the first clause, often inverted: “—,” said she, “—.”
- conduct: Feeling shown by conduct. Feeling given by its effect on conduct or countenance, not named outright: “she could hardly keep her countenance”, “coloured”.
- irony: An ironic verdict. The narrator’s dry verdict on the situation or on the speaker, implied by precision rather than announced; often a generalization (“It is a truth…”, “as is usual…”) or a deflating last clause.

- Keep what the writer named and how much: what was eaten, where, how much (“a whole pint”, “more snacks”) stays, in her words if hers differ. Omit only modern texture that names nothing the story needs (the hoodie).
- Modern things the story needs: a short plain phrase in her words (at most five words), identifiable to the reader, never used twice. Do not make a riddle of it.
- Keep the writer’s degree and register of feeling: a hedge stays a hedge (“basically panicked” is comic, never “exceedingly terrified”), and nothing is compared that was not compared.
- A letter keeps the form of a letter: salutation, the writer’s own voice, and a close; her letters (Lady Susan, the juvenilia, the letters in the novels) are its models.
- Give the passage one ironic verdict in her manner where it invites it: in a first-person passage, the narrator looking back, dry and brief, often in a closing clause or a general observation.
